Introduction

I grew up around aviation, and the thing that stuck wasn't the glamour of it. It was the assumption, built into everything, that the plan you take off with is not necessarily the plan you land with.

A flight has a destination, a route, fuel calculated to the last kilo and a plan worked out to the minute, and yet still is built entirely around the idea that none of it may survive contact with the weather. Runways close. Traffic backs up. Something in the aircraft doesn't behave. Occasionally, the destination you set off towards simply stops being the right place to land.

That way of thinking has started to feel rather relevant to building technology products, especially now. AI has made software dramatically faster and cheaper to build, but faster execution doesn't make the world outside any more predictable. If anything, markets, competitors and what's technically possible can now change faster than most organisations can turn a plan around.

A good product plan can't just describe where you're going. It also has to leave you capable of changing course.

A flight plan is never just a destination. Long before departure, a pilot has already worked out where else the aircraft could go, because if the weather turns, a runway shuts, or an engine fails, there needs to be somewhere else to land, and enough fuel or altitude left to get there.

Startups have a destination too, a particular customer, a product, a workflow, a market, but the risk is building so tightly around that one destination that changing tack later becomes eye-wateringly expensive.

In a world where a competitor can turn up overnight, a model provider can make an entire feature obsolete over a weekend, or customers can simply change their minds faster than the roadmap allows, it's worth asking, quite deliberately: where are our alternate airports?

This isn't an excuse for a wandering strategy. A flight doesn't drift between airports because the crew can't make up their minds, the destination stays clear throughout. But it's worth knowing which adjacent problems, customers or capabilities would become viable if the ground shifted beneath you.

That has architectural consequences too. The best product for today's problem isn't necessarily the most tightly wound implementation you can build. You want enough structure to solve the problem in front of you very well, while leaving sensible seams where the workflow, the data model or the technology can evolve later.

Commercial aviation deliberately splits the job in two: a Pilot Flying and a Pilot Monitoring. One is actively controlling the aircraft. The other is watching the aircraft, the systems, the surroundings, and of course the person flying.

That split exists because whoever is doing the task is usually the worst-placed person to notice everything going wrong around it. There's an obvious product equivalent: the people closest to building something naturally get absorbed in making it work. Someone still has to ask whether it's the right thing to be building, whether the market has shifted, whether users are behaving differently than expected, or whether some earlier technical decision has quietly introduced a risk nobody's watching any more.

None of this requires bureaucracy, or a second team looking over the first one's shoulder. In a small company the two roles might swap between the same pair of people depending on the day. What matters is creating a moment where someone is explicitly responsible for watching rather than doing. Sometimes the most useful thing you can contribute to a project isn't another pair of hands on the controls. It's noticing that the aircraft has started to drift.

The Swiss cheese model describes safety as layers of defence, each one riddled with its own holes. Usually a single weakness doesn't cause a disaster, because another layer catches it. The serious failures happen when several holes happen to line up.

Software behaves the same way. A model misreads a request. The application doesn't check its output. The interface doesn't make the proposed change clear. The user approves it without quite noticing. There's no meaningful undo. None of those, alone, is necessarily catastrophic. All of them together might be.

Which is why I'm wary of any product thinking that tries to solve reliability with a single safeguard. "There's a confirmation button." "We have tests." "The model's very accurate." "We'll keep a human in the loop." None of these is sufficient on its own. The sturdier systems stack several imperfect layers: good defaults, constrained tools, validation, visible consequences, human review, an audit trail, and the ability to undo, and while you won't need every layer for every action, anything consequential shouldn't rest on just one of them going right.

One of the properly counter-intuitive things about aviation is how religiously experienced pilots stick to checklists. Not because they don't know what they're doing but rather because humans are, by nature, inconsistent. Expertise doesn't cure distraction, fatigue, interruption, or a memory that simply drops something under pressure.

There's a useful lesson here for startups, where informality tends to get treated as a virtue in itself. Not everything needs a process. But the things you do repeatedly, and genuinely cannot afford to forget, probably deserve one: deployments, big demos, data migrations, customer onboarding, security reviews, investor materials, important releases.

A lightweight checklist isn't bureaucracy if it reliably stops an expensive mistake. It's simply memory, externalised.

In closing

The point isn't to fly cautiously

Aviation might sound an odd analogy for startups: modern commercial flying is deliberately conservative, and startups are meant to move quickly. But I don't think the useful lesson here is caution. It's prepared adaptability.

A good crew has a destination, they act decisively, they divide the work and they keep watching what's actually happening.

They assume that people and systems will occasionally fail, and they always know where else they could land if reality has other ideas about the original plan.

That feels like a rather useful way to think about building products in 2026. The cost of changing the software is falling fast. The cost of staying loyal to the wrong assumption, however, isn't.

So build for the destination. Just make sure you know where your alternates are.
