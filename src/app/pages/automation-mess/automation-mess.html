Introduction

I built an inventory tool for the university where I work, watched it work perfectly when I tested it, and then not quite so perfectly once other people got their hands on it. That gap taught me more than the tool itself did.

I covered the inventory app I built for the university in a previous article. One of the main decisions I made was to keep it as a simple frontend application: no backend, no database.

The reasoning was straightforward. A database would have meant storing our entire inventory in a new online system, and this wasn't a commercial platform with a team maintaining it, just a small in-house tool built for one specific task. Storing sensitive company information in a new online system would have introduced a risk that wasn't necessary to solve the problem, and one management would quite reasonably have been uncomfortable with. So the app had to stay simple.

Our inventory already existed as a spreadsheet, the same format we'd used in previous years, and the updated inventory ultimately had to go back into our main asset-management platform as a spreadsheet too. The simplest approach was to use that spreadsheet as the source of truth. A user would upload the latest version, scan the assets for their shift, download the updated file, and save it back into our Microsoft 365 environment.

It solved the main bottleneck very well: finding and updating assets manually inside a spreadsheet.

There's a big difference between building and successfully testing a tool yourself, and watching non-technical colleagues use it in the real world.

Despite clear SOPs and step-by-step guides, things went wrong in ways I hadn't fully anticipated.

Some people uploaded an older version of the spreadsheet. Some uploaded an entirely different spreadsheet. A few somehow managed to use the wrong version of the app altogether.

That last one was partly my fault. I'd shared the link to the final application but never disabled an early prototype that was still sitting on an old Claude Artifact link. Six months later, someone found it and used that instead.

Others saved their updated inventory file into the wrong Microsoft 365 folder.

None of this broke the underlying inventory data. But it scattered the work across multiple files, versions and locations. The app had made the physical inventory dramatically faster, days rather than weeks, but it had left behind a small reconciliation problem for me to clean up.

Rather than manually comparing thousands of rows across several Excel files, I built another small application for myself. Its only job was reconciliation: it compared the different inventory files against a master spreadsheet, identified the changes, and helped consolidate everything back into one clean source of truth.

Again, I used AI heavily to build it. But there was no AI inside the application itself. For this job I needed deterministic comparison of thousands of spreadsheet rows, not a language model deciding whether two records "probably" matched.

The result was a clean master inventory and about an hour of reconciliation work. The overall project was still a major improvement on the previous process. But it left me with a more important lesson than the original app did.

When people talk about internal automation, it's easy to think primarily about the workflow: where is time being wasted, what can we automate, what can an engineer build quickly.

AI has made this much more interesting, because small, highly specific pieces of software are now dramatically cheaper and faster to create. I've seen more companies talk about putting technical people directly inside departments like HR, sales, operations or marketing, with the goal of finding and automating these workflows from within. I think there's enormous potential in that model.

But there's a part of it that's easy to underestimate: most of the people using these tools aren't engineers. They may not know, or particularly care, what's happening underneath the interface. They may not understand which file is the source of truth. They may not realise that uploading yesterday's spreadsheet instead of today's creates a completely different outcome. They may see a new internal tool as one more thing to learn rather than an improvement to their workflow. And sometimes they'll simply click the wrong thing.

That isn't a criticism of the user. It's a constraint of the system you're designing.

An engineer tends to test the happy path: upload the right file, click the right button, follow the instructions, save the result where it belongs.

The lesson for me wasn't that lightweight internal software is a bad idea. Quite the opposite, I think we'll see far more of it. The lesson is that building the automation is only part of the work. You also need to think about the mess that imperfect human use will inevitably create.

There's probably a wider transition happening here. Thirty years ago, using a computer or sending an email was still a specialist skill for a large part of the workforce. Today, almost every job involves some level of interaction with software.

I suspect AI-driven tools and small internal applications will follow a similar path. People don't need to become engineers. But over time, basic technical literacy will probably expand: understanding what data a tool is using, what a source of truth is, what an automated workflow can and can't safely do.

Until then, organisations building internal tools need to design around the reality that users will misunderstand them, misuse them, break processes around them, and occasionally create a mess.

In closing

Build the hoover too

None of this is an argument against small, fast, AI-assisted internal tools. If anything, I think we'll build a lot more of them.

So when we build the automation, we should probably build the hoover too.
